# Product Principles

These principles turn our philosophy into decision-making rules. They apply to features, user interfaces, algorithms,
notifications, experiments, and monetization.

The beliefs behind these rules are documented in [philosophy.md](./philosophy.md). Capitalized domain terms use the
definitions in [glossary.md](./glossary.md).

## 1. Optimize for Meaningful Outcomes, Not Engagement

When engagement conflicts with the likelihood of a successful connection, we choose the latter.

Views, messages, and return visits are diagnostic metrics, not evidence of user value.

## 2. Keep Choice Manageable

Recommendations should be limited, predictable, and easy to evaluate.

Recommendation limits exist to reduce choice overload—not to create artificial scarcity, random rewards, or paid
advantages.

## 3. Prefer Quality to Quantity

A small number of relevant Recommendations is better than a large number of weak Candidates.

More Matches are not a success unless they also lead to more Meaningful Conversations and safe Offline Meetings.

## 4. Explain Without False Certainty

Users should understand why someone was recommended and which factors influenced that Recommendation.

Explanations must not expose sensitive information, present assumptions as facts, or promise outcomes.

## 5. Preserve User Agency and Mutuality

The product helps people make decisions—it does not make decisions for them.

Communication begins only after Mutual Interest. Silence, rejection, or ending a Conversation should never be bypassed
through automation or pressure.

## 6. Design Calm, Complete Sessions

Every session should have a natural stopping point.

We avoid infinite scrolling, automatic progression to the next decision, streaks, variable rewards, artificial urgency,
and anxiety-driven notifications.

## 7. Support the Journey After a Match

A Match is the beginning of an opportunity, not the final goal.

The product helps people start a Conversation, align expectations, and safely move toward an Offline Meeting. It does
not build relationships on their behalf or artificially prolong conversations.

## 8. Safety Comes Before Growth

Privacy controls, the ability to end contact, protection of sensitive information, and abuse prevention are launch
requirements—not future improvements.

Growth never justifies weakening these protections.

## 9. Test Hypotheses, Not Just Conversions

Before every experiment, we define the expected Meaningful Outcome, guardrail metrics, and stopping criteria.

An A/B test does not make a manipulative pattern acceptable. Harmful mechanics are never tested simply because they
might improve conversion.

## 10. Never Turn Money into an Advantage Over People

Payment must never increase visibility in hidden rankings, buy another person's attention, or unlock previously hidden
Mutual Interest.

Paid features should provide clear standalone value without making the free experience worse.

## Decision Priority

When principles conflict, decisions should follow this order:

1. Safety and user consent.
2. User well-being.
3. Meaningful Outcomes.
4. Trust, transparency, and data control.
5. Simplicity and accessibility.
6. Product sustainability.
7. Engagement and growth.

## Product Decision Checklist

Before implementing a feature, ask:

1. Which Meaningful Outcome does it improve, and for whom?
2. Could it increase anxiety, compulsive behavior, or choice overload?
3. Does the User remain in control and free to opt out?
4. Is it clear why the system behaves this way and what it does not know?
5. What guardrail metrics and stopping criteria are required?
6. Could we achieve the same outcome while requiring less attention and less user data?

If the first five questions cannot be answered with evidence, the feature is not ready for implementation or
experimentation.
